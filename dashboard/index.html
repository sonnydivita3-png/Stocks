How to read this page

Every company here is scored out of 100 on four things, from the figures it files with the regulator. The scores are then combined into one grade from A to F. A high grade means a company is good at its business, growing, not heavily in debt, and not obviously overpriced today.

A grade is not a prediction. It describes the company as it is now. It cannot know what a business will do next, and the market may already expect everything the numbers show.

Business quality How much profit it earns on the money put into it. Higher is better.
Growth How fast sales are rising, and how steadily rather than in one lucky year.
Financial safety How much it owes, and whether it could survive a bad year.
Price Whether today's share price looks sensible for what the business earns.
Not yet profitable A label on companies still making a loss. Their price score is weak or missing, because there are no profits to value.

Smaller companies growing fast

Companies worth under about ten billion dollars whose sales are growing quickly. This is where a future large company would be found, and also where most of the failures are.

Read this before the list. Most small companies never become large ones. Across the whole history of the US market, the majority of individual stocks returned less than a government savings bond over their lifetime, and almost all of the market's gain came from a small handful of winners. Picking which of these becomes the next big company is not something any screen can do. They are listed because their numbers are interesting, not because they are likely to succeed.

What the scores mean in detail, and what this page cannot tell you

Where the numbers come from

Company figures are taken straight from filings made to the US Securities and Exchange Commission, so each one traces back to a specific filed document and the date it was filed. Share prices, dividends and stock splits come from market data; interest rates and credit spreads from the Federal Reserve. Nothing here uses a figure before the date it was actually published.

How each score is built

Business quality combines return on invested capital averaged over five years, operating and free-cash-flow margins, how much reported profit turns into actual cash, and a nine-point accounting health check.

Growth combines three- and five-year sales growth, how steady that growth has been rather than one good year, and the growth in cash the business generates.

Financial safety combines borrowings against profit, how easily interest is covered, a standard bankruptcy-risk measure, short-term liquidity, and how many of the last seven years produced positive cash.

Price combines what return today's price implies compared with a government bond, the cash the business throws off relative to its market value, how the price compares with a fair value blended across several valuation methods, and where today's valuation sits against the company's own ten-year range.

The overall score weights quality 30%, price 25%, growth 25% and safety 20%. Market momentum is shown separately and deliberately left out of the overall score: it describes what the share price has been doing, not whether the business is sound.

What it cannot tell you

  • It has no forecasts. Everything is extrapolated from what has already been filed, which is a weaker basis than professional estimates for the next year or two.
  • It knows nothing about management, competitors, regulation, lawsuits, or anything written in the footnotes of a filing rather than in the numbers.
  • A company can score well and still be a poor investment, because the price may already reflect everything good about it.
  • Spreading money across many holdings has a far larger effect on most people's results than choosing between individual companies.
  • This is a fixed snapshot taken on one day, not a live feed.
  • It is not investment advice, and it is no substitute for reading a company's own filings or speaking to someone qualified.
