Worth buying now

The grade below is an average of four scores, so a company can be excellent at three things, badly overpriced at the fourth, and still come out with a good grade. That is the right way to ask “is this a sound business?” and the wrong way to ask “should I buy it, at this price?” These lists ask the second question a different way: a set of conditions, every one of which has to hold. Nothing makes up for anything.

There are three lists, because there are three ways a share can be worth buying and they are not judged alike. Asking a fast-growing company for a discount to a no-growth valuation is how you never buy one; asking an unproven company for a five-year profit record rules it out by definition. So each is asked what actually matters for it.

    How to read this page

    Every company here is scored out of 100 on four things, from the figures it files with the regulator. The scores are then combined into one grade from A to F. A high grade means a company is good at its business, growing, not heavily in debt, and not obviously overpriced today.

    A grade is not a prediction. It describes the company as it is now. It cannot know what a business will do next, and the market may already expect everything the numbers show.

    Business quality How much profit it earns on the money put into it. Higher is better.
    Growth How fast sales are rising, and how steadily rather than in one lucky year.
    Financial safety How much it owes, and whether it could survive a bad year.
    Price Whether today's share price looks sensible for what the business earns.
    Fair value estimate What the share looks worth on the cash the business is likely to produce, next to what it costs today. An estimate, not a target.
    Return above Treasury How much more per year you would earn at today's price than by lending the money to the US government, if the business performs as its history suggests.
    What kind of company Each one is labelled: growing fast, a quality compounder, pays an income, steady, shrinking, hard to value, or speculative. The label describes the business, not whether the price is right.
    Not yet profitable A label on companies still making a loss. Their price score is weak or missing, because there are no profits to value.
    What the scores mean in detail, and what this page cannot tell you

    Where the numbers come from

    Company figures are taken straight from filings made to the US Securities and Exchange Commission, so each one traces back to a specific filed document and the date it was filed. Share prices, dividends and stock splits come from market data; interest rates and credit spreads from the Federal Reserve. Nothing here uses a figure before the date it was actually published.

    How each score is built

    Business quality combines return on invested capital averaged over five years, operating and free-cash-flow margins, how much reported profit turns into actual cash, and a nine-point accounting health check. Banks and insurers are scored on return on equity instead, and their cash-flow lines are ignored — see below.

    Growth combines three- and five-year sales growth, how steady that growth has been rather than one good year, and the growth in cash the business generates.

    Financial safety combines borrowings against profit, how easily interest is covered, a standard bankruptcy-risk measure, short-term liquidity, and how many of the last seven years produced positive cash.

    Price combines what return today's price implies compared with a government bond, the cash the business throws off relative to its market value, how the price compares with a fair value blended across several valuation methods, and where today's valuation sits against the company's own ten-year range.

    The overall score weights quality 30%, price 25%, growth 25% and safety 20%. Market momentum is shown separately and deliberately left out of the overall score: it describes what the share price has been doing, not whether the business is sound.

    How “return above Treasury” is worked out

    For an ordinary company it comes from a reverse discounted-cash-flow: given today's price, what annual return would you earn if the business performs roughly as its own history suggests? Subtracting the yield on a 10-year US Treasury gives the extra you are paid for taking equity risk rather than lending to the government.

    That calculation cannot be run on a bank, an insurer or a property trust, because free cash flow to the firm is not defined when borrowing is the raw material. They used to show nothing at all here, which quietly removed most of the financial sector from the comparison. The same question is now put to the model that does fit them: residual income for a bank or insurer, the dividend model for a property trust. The card says which was used.

    Where a share costs more than the model can justify at any sensible required return, the figure reads worse than a number rather than giving a precise one. That boundary is shared by every company past it, so treating it as a measurement would invent agreement that is not there.

    Why insurers no longer fill the top of the list

    The first version of this page put seven insurers in the top fourteen. That was a fault in the scoring, not a finding about insurance. Two things caused it.

    First, an insurer reports almost none of the lines the quality score is built from. There is no meaningful return on invested capital for an insurer, and its free cash flow is dominated by float: premiums arrive years before claims are paid, so cash pours in and says nothing about whether the underwriting was any good. Scoring an insurer on those lines was scoring it on an accounting convention. Financials are now scored on return on equity, which does mean something for them, and their cash-flow lines are dropped rather than flattered.

    Second, and worse, the scores averaged whatever measures happened to exist. A company measured on two favourable lines scored higher than one measured honestly on five — the less that was known about a business, the better it looked. Each score is now pulled toward 50 in proportion to what is missing, so thin evidence produces a middling score rather than a flattering one. Several names that led the first list are now mid-table, which is the point.

    Industry concentration can still be real rather than a bug. Specialty insurance genuinely has had several strong years. Check the sector column before reading a cluster as a coincidence.

    What it cannot tell you

    • It has no forecasts. Everything is extrapolated from what has already been filed, which is a weaker basis than professional estimates for the next year or two.
    • It knows nothing about management, competitors, regulation, lawsuits, or anything written in the footnotes of a filing rather than in the numbers.
    • A company can score well and still be a poor investment, because the price may already reflect everything good about it.
    • Spreading money across many holdings has a far larger effect on most people's results than choosing between individual companies.
    • This is a fixed snapshot taken on one day, not a live feed.
    • It is not investment advice, and it is no substitute for reading a company's own filings or speaking to someone qualified.
